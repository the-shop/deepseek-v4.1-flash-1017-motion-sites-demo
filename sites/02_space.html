01 — The Mission

Eighteen minutes of warning is not a forecast.

Every satellite, every power grid, every polar flight path on Earth sits inside the Sun's weather. We can name a hurricane nine days before it lands. For a coronal mass ejection we get eighteen minutes — and that number has not moved in forty years.

On 4 November 2015 a moderate solar flare blanked out high-frequency radar over the North Atlantic for eleven minutes. Nobody was hurt. A Carrington-class event would find the grid without a spare transformer and the sky without a spare satellite.

Helios-7 flies into the corona instead of around it. For ninety seconds at perihelion it samples the plasma that becomes space weather three days later — at 1 AU, where we live.

"We are not predicting the Sun. We are learning which parts of it to watch."

Dr. Amara Okonkwo — Principal Investigator

02 — The Vehicle

Aurora-class corona probe

  1. 01

    Carbon–carbon thermal shield

    11.4 cm of woven composite, 2.7 m across. The face runs at 1,377 °C at perihelion; the deck eleven centimetres behind it holds at 28 °C.

  2. 02

    Science deck

    Twenty-one instruments on a single optical bench — a wide-field EUV imager, a vector magnetograph, a plasma spectrometer, and the corona camera this mission is named for.

  3. 03

    GaAs solar arrays

    Two 4.2 m² wings producing 1.9 kW at 1 AU. Retracted inside twelve solar radii — at that distance the flux would cook them in under a minute.

  4. 04

    Magnetometer boom

    4.6 m of titanium–zirconium, held rigid to 0.01° and mounted far enough out to escape the spacecraft's own field.

03 — Flight Plan

Three years. Four orbits. One dive.

08 Apr 2027T–00:00:00

Launch

Falcon Heavy, pad LC-39A. Helios-7 departs Cape Canaveral on a hyperbolic escape trajectory at 11.2 km/s, carrying 1,847 kg of spacecraft and a very small mirror.

14 Sep 2027T+ 159 days

Venus flyby I

A 3,000 km pass over Aphrodite Terra sheds 7.4 km/s of heliocentric velocity and tilts the orbital plane by nine degrees. The probe does not photograph Venus. It borrows it.

22 Jan 2028T+ 289 days

First perihelion · 18.4 R☉

The probe crosses inside Mercury's orbit for the first time. Six of twenty-one instruments are powered; the rest stay cold until the shield has proven itself.

11 Aug 2029T+ 856 days

Shield qualification · 9.8 R☉

Peak heating: 1,377 °C on the shield face, 28 °C behind it. The telemetry packet takes nineteen minutes to reach the Deep Space Network dish in Madrid.

03 Mar 2030T+ 1,060 days

Corona transit · 6.2 million km

Ninety seconds inside the corona at 430,000 km/h. The camera records 4,800 frames of a magnetic reconnection event at 0.4 arcseconds per pixel — the best images of a solar storm ever taken.

2030 – 2033Downlink

Data return

At 4.6 kbps from perihelion, the full archive takes fourteen months to deliver. The first three papers land in 2031. The dataset stays open, permanently.
