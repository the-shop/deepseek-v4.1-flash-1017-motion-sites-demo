01 — The baseline

For eleven thousand years the thermostat barely moved. In two lifetimes, we turned it up.

Ice cores pulled from the Antarctic hold air that fell as snow 800,000 years ago. In all of that time, atmospheric CO₂ never climbed past 300 parts per million. Today it reads 424 — a concentration the planet has not seen since before our species existed.

The number at the centre of this story is not a forecast. It is a measurement: 1.30°C of extra heat, averaged across every ocean, every continent and every hour since the industrial revolution. Averaged, because that is the only way a number that large becomes a number that small.

STEP 01 / 05

Every stripe is a year.

Between 1850 and 1900 the global thermometer kept a rhythm. Some years ran warm, some cold, but the average barely drifted. Blue here means cooler than the twentieth-century average. Red means hotter. Watch the right-hand edge.

STEP 02 / 05

The stripes turn red.

The ten warmest years ever measured are the last ten. 2024 closed at 1.55°C above the pre-industrial baseline — the first calendar year on record to cross 1.5. The cool blues now live almost entirely in the nineteenth century.

STEP 03 / 05

The mercury.

One degree of global average is not a warm afternoon. It is energy. The extra heat trapped in the system runs to roughly eight zettajoules a year — more than ten times humanity's entire annual energy consumption, absorbed silently by the oceans.

STEP 04 / 05

No region is average.

Warming is not a blanket. The Arctic is heating nearly four times faster than the global mean. Europe, the fastest-warming continent, has already passed 2.3 degrees. The tropics move slower — and have the most to lose.

STEP 05 / 05

The next degree is a choice.

Every tenth of a degree is a decision about how much carbon we release. The gap between 1.5 and 2.0 is not half a degree of comfort — it is hundreds of millions of people living with heat they cannot escape. The dotted line up there is not a forecast. Yet.

04 — The budget

The last degree
is a budget.

To hold warming at 1.5°C with a coin-flip chance, the world can release roughly 200 billion more tonnes of CO₂. At the current rate of about 41 billion tonnes a year, that is five years of business as usual — a budget already almost entirely spent.
